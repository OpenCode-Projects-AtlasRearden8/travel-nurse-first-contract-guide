# Chapter 3 — Compact Licensing Basics (NLC)

*Education only: licensing rules are set by each state's board of nursing and they change. Always confirm with the board itself and your agency's compliance team. This chapter is background knowledge, not legal advice.*

Before you fall in love with a job in Montana, you need to know whether you are legally allowed to take it — and the **Nurse Licensure Compact (NLC)** is the first and biggest factor.

## What the compact actually is

The NLC is an agreement among participating U.S. states that lets a nurse who holds a **multistate license** issued by their **primary state of residence** work — physically and temporarily — in the other compact states, **without obtaining a new license for each one**.

Key ideas in one breath:

- It is a **multistate license**, not a national license.
- Your eligibility hangs on your **primary state of residence (PSOR)** — where you actually live, vote, register your car, and file taxes, *not* which agency employs you.
- There are **permanent physical presence** and other eligibility requirements defined by your home state's board. The specific rules are set by each board; always read them from the board itself.
- If you have any board discipline or certain legal history in any state, compact eligibility can be affected. Boards decide these case by case.

## Does my state participate?

State lists change (states have joined over the years). Your verification steps, in order:

1. Look up **nursecompact.com** — the compact's official site — for the current list of NLC states.
2. Look up **your own state board of nursing** and read its page on multistate licensing — each board publishes its own exact rules.
3. Ask your agency's compliance department to confirm, in writing, which license the assignment requires.

That is a thirty-minute errand that determines whether you can start next month or need to budget 6–10 weeks for licensure.

## The biggest misunderstanding (worth spelling out)

Holding a multistate license **does not** mean you can telework from a compact state into a non-compact state, or that you can treat any state you visit as temporary. Location of the *patient* and the rules of that state's board are what matter. If you are unsure of an edge case, the board — not the recruiter — is the authority.

Also: a multistate license generally requires you to actually meet residency requirements in your claimed home state. Some states and some employers scrutinize this. Records that show genuine residence (lease, voter registration, tax filing) matter — Chapter 7's record habits cover what to keep.

## If your state is not in the compact

You may need a separate license for each state you work in. Reality check, since you are planning time and money:

- **Processing time:** weeks, sometimes longer; some states are famously slow.
- **Costs:** application fees, fingerprinting, verification fees.
- **Nursys:** the national verification system many boards use to share license data — useful when a second state needs to verify your first license.

Budget both calendar weeks and dollars for this, and tell your recruiter early that licensing — not unit availability — is your critical path.

## A compact assignment, end to end

A realistic first-compact-traveler sequence:

1. **Week 0:** confirm your PSOR and multistate license eligibility with your board.
2. **Weeks 1–2:** shortlist cities and recruiters; verify which states your license covers.
3. **Weeks 2–4:** pick a contract; compliance collects licenses, immunizations, certifications.
4. **Weeks 4–8:** credentialing. The assignment's official start date depends on this.
5. **Week 8:** drive/fly out. Beginner tip: do not accept a Monday start that requires a Sunday-night arrival *across the country*.

## Chapter 3 takeaways

- The NLC is about your **home state's rules**, not the agency's convenience.
- Verify state lists from the compact's site and the board — lists change.
- Non-compact travel is real and fine; it just takes **earlier planning**.
- When in doubt, the board's own website wins over any recruiter's summary.
