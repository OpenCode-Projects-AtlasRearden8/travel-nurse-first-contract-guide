# Chapter 1 — Agency Types, Decoded

The first thing that will strike you about travel nursing is how many companies, portals, and job titles are involved in one thirteen-week gig. This chapter untangles them so the emails in your inbox start making sense.

## The big picture

When you work a travel contract, money flows roughly like this:

> **Hospital** → (pays a bill rate) → **intermediaries** → (pays you, minus their cut) → **you**

Your job as a first-time traveler is not to memorize the plumbing — it is to know *which seat you are sitting in* and *what that means for your pay and protections*.

## The usual players

### National travel agencies

These are the big names you have already heard of. They hold dozens or hundreds of hospital contracts, have dedicated housing teams, and can show you openings across the whole country. **Strengths:** choice, perks programs, experience with first-timers. **Watch-outs:** you are one of many nurses; response speed depends heavily on the recruiter you luck into.

### Boutique and regional agencies

Smaller outfits, often run by former travelers, sometimes focused on one region or one specialty. **Strengths:** personal attention, reusable relationships. **Watch-outs:** fewer job openings on any given day; their accounting and support systems may be lean, so ask direct questions about how pay and housing stipends are handled.

### Local agencies

These staff your *home region*. A local agency is a legitimate way to test travel nursing while sleeping in your own bed — many define "local" as living within a set commute (often 50–60 miles, but ask your agency for its exact rule, because that rule also affects housing stipends). **Strengths:** no uprooting while you learn the ropes. **Watch-outs:** if you live too close, a housing stipend usually does not apply — that money is for nurses who genuinely relocate.

### Managed Service Providers (MSPs) and vendor management systems (VMS)

Here is the part nobody explains on day one. Big hospital systems often hand their entire traveler-staffing function to a **Managed Service Provider (MSP)**, which runs a software **Vendor Management System (VMS)** where agencies compete for open slots. So when you see a posting for a hospital in Texas posted by an agency in Florida running a portal branded with the hospital's name — that is normal. **What it means for you:** your recruiter may be bidding on the job you want, and the *hospital's* rules (cancellation policy, unit requirements) will be baked into the fine print. Ask, in plain words: *"Who actually holds the contract with the hospital — you, or a larger program?"*

### Consultancies and "float pool" style internal programs

Some hospitals run their own internal travel pools and skip agencies entirely. These postings exist too — often on hospital career pages — and can be great, though they tend to be offered locally rather than nationally.

## W2 vs 1099: the seat that changes everything

| | **W2 travel contract** | **1099 "independent contractor"** |
|---|---|---|
| Taxes withheld from paychecks? | Yes | No — you set money aside yourself |
| Employer-side protections generally | More | Fewer |
| Housing stipends & other nontaxable allowances | Common | Less common, more rules |
| Who you really need | A tax pro at filing time | A tax pro *before you sign* |

Most first-time travelers should start with **W2 contracts through an agency**. The 1099 route can make sense eventually, but it hands you the full bookkeeping job on day one. *(Education note: this is general information, not tax advice — a tax professional who knows traveler situations is worth the fee.)*

## Recruiters: one title, many flavors

- **Your recruiter** is the person negotiating with you about pay and perks.
- Sometimes they also handle compliance documents; sometimes a separate **compliance specialist** does.
- At agencies with staffed housing, a **housing coordinator** may exist; at leaner agencies your recruiter does it all.

Practical rule: *interview your recruiter just like they are interviewing you.* You will be texting this person at 8 p.m. on a Sunday when credentialing hits a snag. A good recruiter relationship is worth dollars per hour, genuinely.

## Questions to bring to any agency conversation

(The fully expanded version lives in **Appendix A**.)

1. Who is the actual employer of record — you or a subcontracting agency?
2. Is this a W2 position, and are taxes withheld per paycheck?
3. Is the position inside an MSP/VMS consortium? Who holds the hospital-level contract?
4. What is your company's cancellation policy, and the hospital's?

## Red flags (worth saying out loud)

- Pressure to sign *today* ("the job will close" — most have extensions).
- Guaranteed hours that quietly evaporate in the fine print (Chapter 2).
- A recruiter who cannot or will not answer who the employer of record is.
- Anyone suggesting you "do not file" certain tax forms or work around licensing rules.

## Chapter 1 takeaways

- Know which **type of agency** you are talking to before you talk numbers.
- **W2 first** is the friendlier starting lane for most new travelers.
- The employer of record, cancellation rules, and guarantee hours are the three facts to nail down early.
- You are allowed to interview the recruiter. That is not rudeness; that is prudence.
